## The Literary Roots: What Militsioner Based On in Books

TallBoys has been open about the literary spine of the project. Two novels are repeatedly cited as touchstones, and both deal with the same central question: what happens when an individual collides with an authority that cannot be reasoned with?

| Book | Author | What it contributes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Crime and Punishment* | Fyodor Dostoevsky | Guilt, conscience, and the psychological weight of wrongdoing |
| *The Trial* | Franz Kafka | An opaque accusation, faceless bureaucracy, dread without explanation |

Militsioner based on these two books means the game is less interested in cops-and-robbers action than in the feeling of being watched and judged. Dostoevsky supplies the moral interiority — the sense that the character has done something, or fears they have, and cannot escape their own conscience. Kafka supplies the structural nightmare: a system that accuses you without ever explaining the charge.

That combination explains why the giant policeman is not written as a simple villain. He is closer to a condition than a character — a fact of life in the town, like weather or gravity. You do not defeat him. You avoid him.

### Why the Giant Cop Is Bigger Than a Boss Fight

If you go in expecting a monster to outsmart or a boss to defeat, the literary DNA will read as a mismatch. The size of the policeman is the point: surveillance made physical, scaled up until it becomes the landscape itself. The threat is not violence so much as being *seen*.

## Uncle Styopa, Russophobia, and a Town That Feels Real

When the first trailer appeared on Twitter in September 2020, it spread fast — and not everyone read it the same way. In Russian-speaking media, the giant policeman drew comparisons to Uncle Styopa, the beloved towering Soviet children's-book character who represents the friendly, protective side of policing. Critics argued the game's imagery was Russophobic, flattening a familiar cultural icon into something menacing.

| Reaction | Argument |
| --- | --- |
| Viral enthusiasm | International audiences found the premise surreal, funny, and fresh |
| Russian-language criticism | The giant cop reads as a hostile distortion of a beloved Soviet symbol |
| Developer response | The goal is not an evil police caricature but a story about life in Russia |

Game designer Vladimir Semenets responded directly to that criticism, saying the intent was never to paint police as villains. Instead, he framed the project as a story about life in Russia, and about the themes of responsibility and power — who holds it, who answers for it, and what it costs to live under it.

Militsioner based on real cultural tension rather than pure fantasy is part of why it resonates. The giant policeman is not an invented monster. He is an argument, and the argument is still going.

Inspiration only counts if it reaches the systems. Here is how the themes translate into mechanics players can actually touch.

| Mechanic | Narrative purpose |
| --- | --- |

The microphone feature is the most unusual piece. Conversations can be handled with a microphone, which nudges the game toward performance — you are not just selecting dialogue options, you are participating in a tense social exchange. For a game about being watched and judged, having your own voice in the mix is thematically perfect.

Community reports from the 2025 playtest window treat the game as an experience best approached slowly, and that tracks with everything the inspirations suggest. This is not a sprint to the exit.
